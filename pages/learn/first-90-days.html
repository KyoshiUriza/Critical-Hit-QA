Your First 90 Days

Everything else here optimises for the interview and stops at the offer. This is the part after.

The gap this covers. Passing an interview proves you can answer questions about testing. The job asks something different: read a product nobody has documented, work out what matters, and become useful before anyone's patience runs out. Nobody writes this down, so most people learn it by getting it wrong for a quarter.

Nothing here is about being clever. It is about being the person the team is glad they hired, which is a different and more achievable target.

Week one: access and orientation

Your only real goal in week one is to be able to do the job by the end of it. Access requests take days and nobody chases them for you. Ask on day one, in writing, as a list:

  • The application, in every environment you are expected to test — and the credentials for each.
  • The bug tracker and the test management tool, with permission to create, not just read.
  • The code repositories, including the test suite. Read access is enough at first.
  • CI, so you can see runs and read failures without asking someone to paste them.
  • Logging and monitoring. This is the one people forget to request and then spend a year without.
  • The team's channels, the incident channel, and whatever the release calendar lives in.

Track which are still outstanding and mention them once a week until they land. This reads as organized, not as nagging — and a blocked first month is remembered as a slow start regardless of whose fault it was.

Ask this in your first one-to-one: "What does a good first 90 days look like to you?" It is a fair question, managers rarely get asked it, and the answer tells you what you will actually be judged on — which is often not what the job description said.

Weeks two to four: learn the product before you critique it

The instinct is to start finding bugs immediately, because that is the thing you know how to do. Resist it for a fortnight. Bugs found before you understand the product are usually either intended behavior or already known, and both cost you credibility you have not earned yet.

  1. Use the product as a customer would. Sign up, buy something, cancel it, use it on a phone. Write down every moment you were confused — that list is worth more in week three than it will ever be again, because in a month you will have stopped noticing.
  2. Read the last three months of bugs. Not for the bugs — for the shape. Which areas generate them, what severity language the team uses, what a good report looks like here, who argues about what.
  3. Read the last few incidents. Postmortems tell you what this team is actually afraid of, which is the fastest possible route to knowing what is worth testing.
  4. Run the test suite and read a failure. Even if you will not write tests for months. Knowing how long it takes, how often it is red, and whether anyone trusts it tells you where you are.
  5. Draw the system on one page. Boxes and arrows, however wrong. Show it to an engineer and ask what you got wrong — people who will not read your questions will happily correct your diagram.

Keep a running list of questions rather than asking each one the moment it appears. Batching them into a single conversation respects people's focus, and half of them answer themselves by Friday.

Your first bug report at a new company

It will be read more carefully than any report you file for the next two years, because it is how the team decides what your reports are worth. Before you file it, do three things:

  1. Search the tracker. Filing a known issue as new is the standard first-week mistake and it is entirely avoidable.
  2. Check it is not intended. "Is this deliberate?" asked quietly beats a confident report that turns out to describe the requirement.
  3. Reproduce it twice, on a clean state. Once might be your session, your data, or your extension.

Then match the house style — their severity scale, their template, their level of detail — even where you think yours is better. Adopt the conventions for a quarter and earn the standing to change them later. Arriving and reformatting everything is how a new tester becomes the person the team routes around.

And calibrate down, not up. A new joiner who files their first three findings as critical has spent their credibility before the end of the month. If you are unsure between two bands, take the lower one and state the impact clearly — the evidence will argue it up for you if it deserves it.

When nothing is documented

This is the normal case, not the bad one. Almost no team has current documentation, and expecting it is the fastest way to be unhappy for a quarter. What exists instead:

Instead of docsWhat it tells you
The test suiteThe behavior someone cared enough to pin down. Often the most accurate spec in the building.
Closed ticketsWhat was asked for and what was actually built, including the arguments.
Pull request discussionWhy it was built that way, and what was rejected.
Support ticketsWhat users actually do, which is never what the design assumed.
Analytics and logsWhich paths are hot. Test coverage should roughly follow this and usually does not.
The oldest engineerThe reasons behind everything strange. Ask why, not what — the what you can read.

Then write down what you learn. As a new joiner you are the only person who can see what is missing, and that window closes in about six weeks. A short page on how to set up the environment, or a map of which service does what, is often the single most valued thing a new tester produces in their first quarter — and it is the artifact that gets remembered at your review.

Month two: start contributing

  • Take a real area and own it. Not the whole product. One feature, end to end, well enough that people ask you about it.
  • Join refinement and ask about acceptance criteria. "How would we know this works?" is the highest-value sentence a tester says, and it is worth more before the code exists than after.
  • Fix one thing about the suite. A flaky test, a slow one, a missing check for a bug that shipped. Small and finished beats a proposal to rewrite it.
  • Automate something you have done manually three times. Three is the threshold where the effort pays and where you understand it well enough to automate correctly.
  • Say what you are not covering. A tester who names the gaps is trusted far more than one who implies everything is fine.

If your first automated test lands in month two rather than week two, that is normal and usually better. A test written before you understand the product tends to assert the current behavior, which is exactly the failure the Code Review Gauntlet's generated exercises are about.

Month three: the 90-day conversation

Most places have some version of a probation or 90-day review. Walk into it with your own summary rather than waiting to be assessed:

  • What you shipped. Bugs found and their impact, tests written, documentation created, releases supported. Numbers where you have them.
  • What you learned. Areas you can now work independently, and — honestly — the ones you still cannot.
  • What you noticed. The observations from week three, now that you can say which ones matter. This is where a new joiner's value is highest and it expires.
  • What you want next. A specific area, skill, or responsibility. Managers can act on specific.

Keep a running note from week one. Reconstructing three months from memory in the week before the review produces a thinner and less accurate story than the one you actually lived.

Traps that catch new joiners

  • Comparing everything to your last job. "At my last company we…" is useful once a month and corrosive once a day. Understand why it is done this way before proposing that it is not.
  • Waiting to be told what to test. Nobody is coming with a plan. Pick an area, say what you are going to do, and do it.
  • Hiding confusion. The window where "I don't understand this yet" costs you nothing is roughly your first month, and it never reopens. Spend it.
  • Being the quality police. Blocking releases and counting other people's mistakes makes you an obstacle. Testers with influence got it by being useful, not by being right.
  • Rewriting the framework in month one. It is almost always the wrong call, and it is always read as a judgment on whoever wrote it.
  • Going quiet when blocked. A day stuck is fine. A week stuck and silent is the thing that ends probations.
The one habit worth more than the rest of this page: write down what you were confused by, in your first month, before you stop being confused. It becomes your onboarding document, your first real contribution, and the reason the next person has an easier start than you did.
