Code Review Gauntlet

The hands-on round most automation interviews actually run: here is a test, tell me what is wrong with it.

How this is scored — read this first

You get a snippet and a list of possible criticisms. Tick the ones that genuinely apply. Some of them do not.

Both directions count. Missing a real defect costs you, and flagging clean code costs you too — because in a real review that is the more expensive mistake. An engineer who objects to everything gets their comments discounted, and interviewers are watching for exactly that. Ticking every box will score badly on purpose.

Snippets are Playwright in JavaScript, but the defects — sleeps, shared state, brittle locators, assertions that cannot fail — are the same in Cypress, Selenium, and any framework you will be handed.

    What interviewers are really checking
    
      
    The snippet is a prompt. The signal they are reading is how you talk about it.
    
      
      
        
    • Do you name the failure mode, or just the rule? "Don't use sleeps" is a rule. "This is 5s slower than it needs to be and still fails on a slow runner" is understanding.
      • 
        
    • Do you prioritise? A test that cannot fail matters more than an untidy screenshot call. Leading with the nit signals you cannot tell them apart.
      • 
        
    • Do you separate defects from preferences? Saying "nit:" out loud is a senior habit and costs nothing.
      • 
        
    • Can you say why the fix is better rather than just what to change? "Auto-retrying assertions wait exactly as long as needed" beats "use expect instead".
      • 
        
    • Are you kind about it? They are imagining you reviewing their team's code. Blunt is fine; contemptuous is disqualifying.
      • 
      
    
      
    
        If you are asked to review live and you spot nothing, say what you would
        check and why — reading order, then assertions, then locators, then
        state. A visible method beats silence.
