Why this is now a hiring requirement

The European Accessibility Act took effect in June 2025, extending obligations from public-sector bodies to a wide range of private products and services. In the US, ADA-based litigation over websites has run into the thousands of filings a year for several years running.

The practical consequence for you: accessibility has moved from "something the design team cares about" to mandatory test coverage with a compliance deadline. Teams that had no accessibility testing now need it, and the people who can do it are thin on the ground. That is unusually good news for someone entering the field — it is a scarce skill you can demonstrate without permission from an employer.

The standard you will be asked about is WCAG 2.1 Level AA. Not AAA — that is aspirational and rarely required. Not 2.0 — superseded. If a job description says "WCAG compliance" and nothing else, AA is what they mean, and asking which level and version is a good question to ask them.

What tools catch — and the number that matters

Automated accessibility tools — axe, Lighthouse, WAVE, Pa11y — are fast, free, and worth running on every page. They also detect roughly a third of WCAG issues. Deque, who build axe, publish this themselves rather than hiding it.

Knowing which third is the whole skill. Roughly:

A tool reliably catchesA tool cannot judge
Missing alt attributes Whether the alt text describes the image
Text contrast below the ratio Whether meaning is carried by color alone
Form inputs with no programmatic label Whether the label makes sense to a human
Missing document language, duplicate ids Whether focus order matches the visual order
ARIA attributes that are invalid Whether ARIA was needed, or is actively lying
Empty buttons and links Whether a custom widget is operable by keyboard

The right-hand column is where the serious defects live, and every one of them needs a person. "We run axe in CI" is a good answer to half the question; the interviewer is waiting to hear what you do about the other half.

The failure mode to name in an interview: a page that passes an automated scan and is unusable. An image labeled alt="image" passes. A div with role="button" and no key handler passes. A perfect scan with a broken keyboard path is common, and it is exactly why the automated number is a floor rather than a result.

The keyboard pass — ten minutes, finds the most

If you do one accessibility check, do this one. Put the mouse down and drive the page with Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, the arrow keys and Esc.

  1. Can you reach everything interactive? Anything you can click with a mouse and cannot reach with Tab is a failure of WCAG 2.1.1. Custom controls built from divs are the usual culprit.
  2. Can you see where you are? Every focused element needs a visible indicator (2.4.7). outline: none with nothing put back is the single most common accessibility defect on the web.
  3. Does focus order match the visual order? If Tab jumps from the top of the page to the footer and back, something is reordered in CSS or has a positive tabindex.
  4. Can you get out of everything? Open a modal, a date picker, a custom dropdown — can you leave with Esc, and does focus return sensibly? A focus trap you cannot escape (2.1.2) is severe: a keyboard user is stuck on the page.
  5. Does Enter and Space do what they should? A real button responds to both. A div pretending to be one usually responds to neither.
  6. Is there a way to skip the navigation? Tab once from the top of a page — a skip link should appear (2.4.1). This site has one; press Tab now and watch.

None of this needs a tool, an extension, or permission. It is the highest-value ten minutes in testing, and most candidates have never done it once.

The screen reader pass

You do not need to be a proficient screen reader user to find defects with one. You need to listen for the gap between what is announced and what is on screen.

What is already on your machine:

  • Windows — Narrator (Ctrl+Win+Enter), or NVDA, which is free and what most testers use.
  • macOS — VoiceOver (Cmd+F5).
  • iOS / Android — VoiceOver / TalkBack, in accessibility settings.

The four things to listen for:

  1. Does every control announce a purpose? "Button" on its own is a defect — a button whose only content is an icon needs an accessible name.
  2. Is the page navigable by heading? Screen reader users jump by heading rather than reading top to bottom. Skipped levels and headings used for styling break that.
  3. Are errors announced? A validation message that appears silently does not exist for a screen reader user. It needs role="alert" or a live region.
  4. Do images say something useful? Decorative images should be silent (alt=""). Informative ones should describe the information, not the picture.
The most useful hour you can spend: turn on a screen reader, close your eyes, and try to complete a purchase on a site you did not build. You will not forget what you learn, and it is a far better interview answer than any definition of WCAG.

Contrast, computed properly

The thresholds are worth memorising because they come up constantly:

WhatAA ratioCriterion
Body text4.5:11.4.3
Large text (18.66px bold, or 24px)3:11.4.3
UI component boundaries and states3:11.4.11
Focus indicators3:1 against adjacent colors2.4.11

Two traps that catch people who think they have this covered:

  • Translucent colors composite. A focus ring at rgba(79,157,255,0.55) is not the ratio of that blue — it is the ratio of that blue blended with whatever is behind it. This site shipped exactly that defect: a ring whose own code comment claimed it passed, measuring 2.85:1 in reality. It is fixed, and the story is in the stylesheet.
  • Disabled controls are exempt, and that is often misused. WCAG excludes disabled elements from 1.4.3. That does not make an unreadable disabled button good design, and if users cannot tell whether a control is disabled or merely low-contrast, you have a usability defect even without a WCAG one.
This site computes its own. Its test suite measures every color pair against the real sRGB luminance formula, including alpha compositing for translucent rings — tests/contrast.spec.js and tests/focus-contrast.spec.js in the repository. They are short, readable, and a working example of automating the part of accessibility that genuinely can be automated.

Writing an accessibility bug report

An accessibility report that says "this is not accessible" gets deprioritised. One that names the criterion, the user, and the consequence gets fixed.

Weak

"The subscribe button doesn't work with the keyboard. Should be fixed."

Actionable

"Subscribe cannot be activated by keyboard — WCAG 2.1.1 (A). It is a <div> with a click handler, no role, no tabindex and no key handler, so Tab skips it and Enter does nothing. Any keyboard or screen-reader user is unable to subscribe at all. Fix: use a <button>."

Four things to include every time:

  1. The criterion and level — "2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A)". It converts an opinion into a standard, and Level A failures are hard to argue down.
  2. Who is blocked — keyboard-only, screen reader, low vision, color blind, motor impairment. Naming the user makes the impact concrete.
  3. Whether there is a workaround. "Blocked entirely" and "awkward but possible" are different severities, and conflating them costs you credibility.
  4. The fix, if it is obvious. Very often it is one semantic element. Saying so gets it done in the same sprint.

On severity: a Level A failure that blocks a task should not be filed as Low because "only a few users hit it". That reasoning does not survive contact with a legal team, and increasingly does not survive contact with a regulator.

What interviewers ask

  • "How would you test a page for accessibility?" The answer they want is a process: automated scan first for the cheap wins, then a keyboard pass, then a screen reader pass on the critical paths, then contrast on the design system. Leading with "I'd run Lighthouse" and stopping is the common weak answer.
  • "What percentage does an automated tool catch?" Around a third. Knowing this specific number, and that Deque publish it, signals you have read past the marketing.
  • "What's the difference between AA and AAA?" AA is the compliance target almost everyone uses; AAA is stricter (7:1 contrast, sign language for video) and rarely required wholesale.
  • "When would you use ARIA?" As little as possible. The first rule of ARIA is not to use ARIA — a native <button> beats role="button" every time. Bad ARIA is worse than none, because it lies to assistive technology confidently.
  • "Give an example of something a tool cannot catch." Have one ready. Focus order not matching visual order, or alt text that exists and describes nothing, are both good.

Practice — earn Star-Dust

  1. Work the Accessibility Challenge with the keyboard only, before you inspect anything. Thirteen defects are seeded and now cataloged — see how many the keyboard alone reveals.
  2. Repeat it with a screen reader on. Different defects surface.
  3. Run an automated scan over the same page and compare: which of the thirteen did it find? That comparison is the lesson.
  4. Write one of them up in the Bug Report Builder using the four-part structure above, citing the criterion.
  5. Take the Manual quiz — accessibility questions are in there.
